Reading — Inference

8 questions
Artificial intelligence has begun to reshape the practice of science itself. Algorithms can now sift through decades of published research in minutes, flagging patterns that might take a human researcher years to notice — and, proponents argue, this could dramatically shorten the path from hypothesis to discovery. Yet the same systems that promise speed also introduce a curious blind spot: a model trained on existing literature will, almost by definition, be better at confirming what is already suspected than at recognising the genuinely unexpected. Some laboratories have begun treating AI-generated hypotheses with the same scepticism once reserved for anecdotal evidence, insisting on independent replication before any claim is taken seriously. Whether this caution will hold as funding pressures mount, and as the temptation to publish faster grows, is far from certain. What is clear is that the scientists best placed to benefit from these tools will not be those who trust them uncritically, but those who understand precisely where their judgement is still required.
None of these answers are stated directly — you'll need to infer them from tone, word choice and structure.

Writing — Risk Memo

200–250 words
Task: Write a short risk-summary memo to a non-technical company director, explaining the risks and benefits of introducing an AI-assisted hypothesis-generation tool into your R&D lab. The director is busy, mildly sceptical of new technology, and will only read one page.
  1. Open with the purpose of the memo in one sentence.
  2. Briefly state the main benefit.
  3. Briefly state the main risk.
  4. Address the director's likely scepticism directly.
  5. Avoid technical jargon — write for a non-specialist.
  6. End with a clear, single recommendation.
