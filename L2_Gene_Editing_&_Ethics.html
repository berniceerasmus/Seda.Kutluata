Reading — Inference

8 questions
Few technologies have advanced as quickly, or raised as many questions, as gene editing. What was once confined to specialist laboratories can now, in principle, be attempted with equipment costing little more than a few thousand dollars. Advocates point to the medical potential — heritable diseases eliminated before birth, crops engineered to withstand a changing climate — and argue that excessive caution carries its own cost, measured in suffering that earlier intervention could have prevented. Critics counter that the technology's accessibility is precisely the problem: a tool once reserved for institutions with ethics boards and years of oversight is now within reach of anyone with sufficient technical skill and rather less patience for scrutiny. Between these positions sits a quieter, more uncomfortable question, one that neither side addresses directly: whether the pace of regulatory debate can plausibly keep up with the pace of the science it is meant to govern. If it cannot, the real decision may already have been made — not by ethicists or lawmakers, but by whoever reaches the laboratory bench first.
None of these answers are stated directly — you'll need to infer them from tone, word choice and structure.

Writing — Position Paper

200–250 words
Task: Write a position paper for an internal company ethics committee, arguing either for or against your organisation investing in gene-editing research. The committee values balanced reasoning over passion.
  1. State your position clearly in the opening sentence.
  2. Give your strongest supporting reason.
  3. Acknowledge the strongest counter-argument honestly.
  4. Explain why your position still holds despite that counter-argument.
  5. Avoid emotive language — keep the tone measured and professional.
  6. End with a clear, one-sentence conclusion or recommendation.
